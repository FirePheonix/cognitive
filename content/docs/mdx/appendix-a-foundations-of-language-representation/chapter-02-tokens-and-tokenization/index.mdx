# 2. Tokens and Tokenization

Let's start with a concrete question: when you type a message to ChatGPT, what does the model *actually receive*?

Not your words. Not your letters. Not even bytes.

It receives a sequence of **integers** — a list of numbers like `[791, 43678, 374, 279, 8136, 315, 1475, 6617, 445, 44, 13]`.

That's the sentence `"The transformer is the engine of every modern LLM."` after it has been processed by a tokenizer. Eleven integers representing eleven pieces of text. The model has no direct access to the original characters — it only ever sees these IDs.

Run the cell below to see this yourself with GPT-4's real tokenizer.

Notice a few things right away: most words map to one token, but `LLM` splits into `LL` and `M`. The leading space before words like ` transformer` is part of the token. And the period at the end is its own token. This isn't random — it's the result of a specific algorithm that decided which character combinations are common enough to become single tokens.

Understanding how text becomes those integers is the first step to understanding everything else about how language models work.

---

## What Is a Token?

A **token** is the smallest unit of text that a language model works with.

It might be a whole word. It might be part of a word. It might be a punctuation mark, a space, or even a single character. The exact definition depends on which tokenizer you're using, but the principle is always the same: text gets chopped into these pieces, and each piece gets assigned an integer ID from a fixed lookup table called the **vocabulary**.

The process works in two directions:

- **Encode**: `text → sequence of integer IDs`
- **Decode**: `sequence of integer IDs → text`

Here's the thing to hold onto: **the integer IDs are just labels.** Token ID 791 doesn't mean "the number 791" in any mathematical sense. It's like an index in a dictionary — it tells you which row to look up in the embedding table. The value itself is arbitrary.

---

## Three Ways to Tokenize Text

Before we get to how modern LLMs do it, let's work through the three main approaches — because understanding why the first two fall short is what makes the third one click.

### Approach 1: Character Level

The most straightforward idea: every single character is its own token.

```
"cat" → ['c', 'a', 't'] → [2, 0, 16]
```

The vocabulary is tiny — just the characters in your training data. For English, that's roughly 70–100 symbols. No such thing as an "unknown" character, because any new text is still made of characters you've seen.

Sounds clean. But there's a catch: **sentences get very long.** A 100-word passage might be 500 characters — 500 tokens. And there's a semantic problem: the model has to learn from scratch that `c-a-t` together represent a concept, rather than just having one token for "cat" that it can learn directly.

Let's build one and see exactly what happens.

Seven characters, seven tokens. The vocabulary is tiny (20 entries), but we got zero compression — a long document becomes a very long sequence. The model will struggle to pay attention across such long sequences.

### Approach 2: Word Level

The other extreme: each word is its own token.

```
"the cat sat" → ['the', 'cat', 'sat'] → [14, 2, 11]
```

Shorter sequences, and each token maps directly to a meaningful unit. But now there's a **vocabulary explosion problem** — real text has an effectively unlimited number of word forms: "cat", "cats", "catlike", "catnap", "catnapping". Plus proper nouns, typos, technical terms, new slang. You can never put them all in a vocabulary.

The moment you encounter a word you've never seen: **OOV — out of vocabulary.** The tokenizer has no ID to give it.

```notebook
    {
```

So we're stuck between two bad options. Character-level has no OOV problem but produces absurdly long sequences. Word-level has short sequences but falls apart on anything it hasn't seen. What we actually need is something in between.

## BPE: The Algorithm That Actually Works

Subword tokenization — specifically **Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)** — solves both problems at once. The idea: keep common words whole, but split rare ones into smaller known pieces.

```
"playing"      → ['play', 'ing']
"unbelievable" → ['un', 'believ', 'able']
"the"          → ['the']           ← common, kept whole
"cats"         → ['cat', 's']      ← split into known pieces
```

How does the algorithm decide what to merge? It's beautifully simple: **start with characters, find the most frequent adjacent pair, merge it into a new token, repeat.**

Let's watch it happen step by step. The three pictures below use a tiny corpus — just the words `the the` — so you can count the pairs by hand. The notebook after that runs the same algorithm on a slightly larger set of sentences.

![BPE step 1. Corpus “the the” starts as seven character tokens. Adjacent pair counts: t h and h e each appear twice; e-space and space-t appear once. Frequency, not linguistics, picks the next merge.](/appendix-a/02-tokens/bpe-step-1-count-pairs.png)

![BPE step 2. After merging t + h → th, the stream is th, e, space, th, e. The new winner is th + e → the, which is how the algorithm discovers the English word “the” from counts alone.](/appendix-a/02-tokens/bpe-step-2-merge.png)

![BPE step 3. After the second merge the corpus is two copies of the token “the”. Encoding the unseen word “then” applies the same rules in order: letters → th e n → the n. No unknown-word crash.](/appendix-a/02-tokens/bpe-step-3-encode-unseen.png)

```notebook
  "runtime": "Python",

    {
      "id": "cell-bpe-train",
      "type": "code",
      "source": "class BPETokenizer:\n    def __init__(self):\n        self.merge_rules = []  # ordered list of (a, b) pairs\n        self.stoi = {}\n        self.itos = {}\n\n    def train(self, texts, num_merges=12):\n        # Step 0: every sentence starts as a list of individual characters\n        token_lists = [list(text) for text in texts]\n        vocab = set(ch for tl in token_lists for ch in tl)\n\n        print(f'Start: {len(vocab)} base characters')\n        print(f'  {sorted(vocab)}')\n        print()\n\n        for step in range(num_merges):\n            # Count every adjacent pair across all sentences\n            pairs = {}\n            for tl in token_lists:\n                for i in range(len(tl) - 1):\n                    pair = (tl[i], tl[i+1])\n                    pairs[pair] = pairs.get(pair, 0) + 1\n\n            if not pairs:\n                break\n\n            # Pick the most frequent pair\n            best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)\n            a, b = best\n            new_tok = a + b\n            self.merge_rules.append(best)\n            vocab.add(new_tok)\n\n            # Apply the merge everywhere in the corpus\n            new_lists = []\n            for tl in token_lists:\n                new_tl, i = [], 0\n                while i < len(tl):\n                    if i < len(tl)-1 and tl[i] == a and tl[i+1] == b:\n                        new_tl.append(new_tok)\n                        i += 2\n                    else:\n                        new_tl.append(tl[i])\n                        i += 1\n                new_lists.append(new_tl)\n            token_lists = new_lists\n\n            print(f'  Merge {step+1:2d}: {a!r} + {b!r} → {new_tok!r}'\n                  f'   (appeared {pairs[best]}x)   vocab now: {len(vocab)}')\n\n        # Build vocabulary lookup tables\n        sorted_vocab = sorted(vocab)\n        self.stoi = {t: i for i, t in enumerate(sorted_vocab)}\n        self.itos = {i: t for i, t in enumerate(sorted_vocab)}\n        print(f'\\nDone. Final vocabulary: {len(vocab)} tokens.')\n\n    def encode(self, text):\n        tokens = list(text)\n        for a, b in self.merge_rules:\n            i, out = 0, []\n            while i < len(tokens):\n                if i < len(tokens)-1 and tokens[i] == a and tokens[i+1] == b:\n                    out.append(a + b)\n                    i += 2\n                else:\n                    out.append(tokens[i])\n                    i += 1\n            tokens = out\n        return [self.stoi[t] for t in tokens]\n\n    def decode(self, ids):\n        return ''.join(self.itos[i] for i in ids)\n\n\nbpe = BPETokenizer()\nbpe.train(corpus)",
      "outputs": [
        {
          "type": "text",
          "content": "Start: 20 base characters\n  [' ', 'a', 'c', 'd', 'e', 'f', 'g', 'h', 'i', 'l', 'm', 'n', 'o', 'p', 'r', 's', 't', 'u', 'v', 'y']\n\n  Merge  1: 'e' + ' ' → 'e '   (appeared 14x)   vocab now: 21\n  Merge  2: 't' + 'h' → 'th'   (appeared 12x)   vocab now: 22\n  Merge  3: 'th' + 'e ' → 'the '   (appeared 10x)   vocab now: 23\n  Merge  4: 'a' + 't' → 'at'   (appeared 8x)   vocab now: 24\n  Merge  5: 'o' + 'g' → 'og'   (appeared 6x)   vocab now: 25\n  Merge  6: 'at' + ' ' → 'at '   (appeared 5x)   vocab now: 26\n  Merge  7: 's' + ' ' → 's '   (appeared 5x)   vocab now: 27\n  Merge  8: 'd' + 'og' → 'dog'   (appeared 4x)   vocab now: 28\n  Merge  9: 'c' + 'at' → 'cat'   (appeared 4x)   vocab now: 29\n  Merge 10: 'l' + 'og' → 'log'   (appeared 2x)   vocab now: 30\n  Merge 11: 'm' + 'at' → 'mat'   (appeared 2x)   vocab now: 31\n  Merge 12: 'i' + ' ' → 'i '   (appeared 2x)   vocab now: 32\n\nDone. Final vocabulary: 32 tokens."
        }
      ]
    }
  ]
}
```

![How tokenization is not word-by-word](/appendix-a/02-tokens/how-tokenization-is-not-word-by-word.jpeg)

Watch what happened. The algorithm didn't need to be told that "cat" or "dog" are meaningful words — it figured that out purely from frequency. `e + ' '` merged first because word endings appear constantly. Then `t + h` because "the" dominates English. By merge 9, the algorithm had independently discovered that `c-a-t` belongs together.

This is the whole trick: frequency in real language reflects meaning, and BPE captures that.

## The Weird Things Tokenization Causes

Once you understand how tokenization works, a bunch of language model behaviors that seem mysterious start to make sense.

**Why do LLMs sometimes struggle with simple spelling?**

Ask "how many r's are in strawberry?" and GPT-3.5 famously got it wrong. Why? Because the model doesn't see `s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y` as individual letters. "straw" is probably one token, "berry" another. The individual characters aren't explicitly present — the model has to *reason* about what characters a token contains, which is harder than it looks.

**Why do models struggle with decimal comparisons?**

We saw it above: `1.11` tokenizes as `1`, `.`, `11` and `1.9` as `1`, `.`, `9`. The model sees the fragments `11` and `9` and might naively think `11 > 9`, therefore `1.11 > 1.9`. Mathematically wrong. It has to learn decimal alignment rules from how these tokens appear in training text — that's a harder lesson than just comparing numbers.

**Why does " cat" tokenize differently from "cat"?**

The leading space is part of the token. ` cat` (space-cat) and `cat` (no space) are different tokens with different IDs, because most words appear mid-sentence after a space. The model learned them separately.

**Why is model quality uneven across languages?**

English compresses to roughly 4–5 characters per token. Chinese might be 1.5–2 (multi-byte characters split less efficiently with byte-level BPE). A 4096-token context holds much more English text than Chinese. And most training data was in English, so the English vocabulary is denser and more efficient.

## Quick Recap

- A **token** is the basic unit a language model works with — could be a word, word-piece, character, or special symbol.
- **Tokenization** converts text into a sequence of integer IDs via a fixed lookup table (the vocabulary).
- **Character-level**: tiny vocab, zero OOV, but very long sequences and fragmented semantics.
- **Word-level**: short sequences, but crashes on anything unseen (OOV problem).
- **BPE**: start with characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair. Common words become single tokens; rare words decompose into known fragments. No OOV.
- Industrial tokenizers use **bytes** as base units for universal Unicode coverage.
- **Special tokens** are hardcoded control signals — not learned from frequency — used for things like conversation structure.
- Token IDs are **just indices** — no intrinsic meaning, just pointers into the embedding table.
