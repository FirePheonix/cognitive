# 11. Attention — The Fix for the Bottleneck

Chapter 10 ended on an uncomfortable fact. A seq2seq model reads the whole source sentence and then has to squeeze everything it read into **one fixed-size vector** before the decoder writes a single word. A five-word sentence and a fifty-word sentence get the same 1,000 numbers. Cho et al. (2014) measured what you would expect: translation quality held up for short sentences and fell steadily once they grew past about 20 words.

In 2014, Dzmitry Bahdanau, then an intern in Yoshua Bengio's lab, asked a simple question: *why make the decoder work from memory at all?* The encoder has already produced one hidden state per source word. Keep all of them, and at every step let the decoder **look back** and choose which ones it needs right now. The paper — "Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate" (Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio, ICLR 2015) — called this soft alignment. Everyone else came to call it **attention**, and three years later it became the only thing left in the Transformer.

This chapter builds that mechanism from the ground up: the equations, a worked example you can check by hand, the variants that followed, and a small model you can train yourself to watch attention discover an alignment no one programmed.

## The idea in one picture

Picture a human translator working on "The agreement on the European Economic Area was signed in August 1992." They don't memorise the sentence, close the book and write French. While writing each French word they glance back at the part of the English that matters for it. When they get to *zone* they are looking at *Area*; for *européenne* they look back at *European*.

Attention gives the decoder exactly that glance. At every output step it:

1. **scores** every source position — "how useful is word $j$ for what I'm about to write?";
2. turns the scores into **weights** that are positive and sum to 1 (a softmax);
3. builds a **context vector**: the weighted average of the encoder states;
4. uses that context, together with its own state, to produce the next word.

The context vector is rebuilt at every step, so the decoder is never forced to rely on one summary of the entire sentence.

## The math, one line at a time

The notation follows the original paper. Source words are $x_1, \dots, x_{T_x}$, the decoder writes $y_1, y_2, \dots$, and $t$ counts decoder steps.

### 1. Annotate every source word (bidirectional encoder)

A left-to-right RNN state $\overrightarrow{h}_j$ only knows the words up to $j$. Bahdanau runs a second RNN right-to-left and concatenates the two, so each **annotation** summarises the whole sentence with the focus on word $j$:

$$
h_j = \left[\, \overrightarrow{h}_j \,;\, \overleftarrow{h}_j \,\right]
$$

This detail matters more than it looks. When the decoder later "looks at" position $j$, it isn't looking at one isolated word — it's looking at that word *in context*.

### 2. Score each annotation against the decoder's state

The decoder's previous state $s_{t-1}$ encodes what it has written so far and, implicitly, what it needs next. A tiny one-hidden-layer network — the **alignment model** — scores how well each annotation fits:

$$
e_{tj} = v_a^{\top} \tanh\!\left( W_a\, s_{t-1} + U_a\, h_j \right)
$$

$W_a$ and $U_a$ project both vectors into a shared space, $\tanh$ lets them interact, and $v_a$ collapses the result to one number. Because the two projections are **added**, this is called **additive attention**. Note that $U_a h_j$ doesn't depend on $t$, so it can be computed once per sentence and reused at every step.

### 3. Turn scores into weights

$$
\alpha_{tj} = \frac{\exp(e_{tj})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{tk})}
$$

Every $\alpha_{tj}$ is positive and they sum to 1 over the source — a probability distribution over "where to look".

### 4. Build the context vector

$$
c_t = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{tj}\, h_j
$$

A weighted average of annotations. If one weight is close to 1, $c_t$ is essentially that one annotation; if the weights are spread out, it blends several.

### 5. Update the decoder and predict

$$
s_t = f\!\left(s_{t-1},\; y_{t-1},\; c_t\right), \qquad p(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x) = g\!\left(y_{t-1},\; s_t,\; c_t\right)
$$

$f$ is a GRU cell; $g$ is a small output network ending in a softmax over the vocabulary. The only change from Chapter 10 is that the context is now $c_t$ — different at every step — instead of a single fixed $c$.

Everything here is differentiable, so the alignment model is trained jointly with the encoder and decoder from nothing but translation pairs. No one ever supplies the "right" alignment.

### Worked example — check it by hand

Take the step where the decoder writes *zone*, with scores against "The European Economic Area" of $e = [0.2,\ 0.9,\ 1.6,\ 3.9]$.

| | The | European | Economic | Area |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| score $e_j$ | 0.2 | 0.9 | 1.6 | 3.9 |
| $\exp(e_j)$ | 1.22 | 2.46 | 4.95 | 49.40 |
| weight $\alpha_j$ = exp ÷ 58.03 | **0.02** | **0.04** | **0.09** | **0.85** |

So $c_t \approx 0.02\,h_1 + 0.04\,h_2 + 0.09\,h_3 + 0.85\,h_4$ — mostly *Area*. Notice how the exponential amplifies differences: a score only 2.3 higher than the runner-up wins 85% of the weight.

## What the weights revealed

Bahdanau's most striking figure wasn't the BLEU score; it was a picture. Plot $\alpha_{tj}$ as a grid — French output words down the side, English source words across the top — and the trained model's weights trace out a **word alignment**, the thing statistical translation systems had needed separate, hand-engineered models to estimate.

![Attention alignment for "The agreement on the European Economic Area was signed in August 1992" translated to "L'accord sur la zone économique européenne a été signé en août 1992", redrawn from Figure 3 of Bahdanau et al. 2015. Mostly a diagonal, with a reversed block where "European Economic Area" becomes "zone économique européenne". Side notes point out the reversal, a many-to-one case ("a été signé" spread over "was signed"), and that soft attention spreads weight rather than picking a single word.](/appendix-c/11-attention-the-fix-for-the-bottleneck/alignment-heatmap.png)

Three things stand out:

- **Mostly diagonal.** English and French usually keep the same word order, so output word $t$ looks near source word $t$.
- **A reversed block.** French puts the adjective after the noun, so "European Economic Area" becomes "zone économique européenne". The weights flip to follow it. Nothing told the model about adjective order; it discovered the alignment because looking in the right place made the translation loss go down.
- **Soft, not hard.** Where one English word maps to several French words, or the reverse ("was signed" → "a été signé"), the weight spreads across neighbours. A hard alignment would have to pick one; a weighted average doesn't.

### Does it actually help?

Bahdanau trained the same encoder–decoder with and without attention, on sentences up to 30 or 50 words, and reported BLEU on English→French (WMT'14, Table 1 of the paper):

| model | trained on ≤ 30 words | trained on ≤ 50 words |
|---|---|---|
| RNNencdec (fixed context vector) | 13.93 | 17.82 |
| **RNNsearch (attention)** | **21.50** | **26.75** |

Nearly 9 BLEU points at the same size and data. More telling was the paper's plot of BLEU against sentence length: the fixed-vector model collapsed on long sentences, while RNNsearch-50 barely dropped. The bottleneck was gone.

## Train it yourself: watch an alignment appear

Real translation data takes days to train on. **Reversing a string** isolates the same problem in a couple of minutes on a laptop CPU: given `c g a b i b f j`, output `j f b i b a g c`. Output letter $t$ must come from source position $n-1-t$, so the "correct" alignment is a perfect anti-diagonal, and a fixed-context model has to memorise the entire string in one vector.

The notebook trains two otherwise identical seq2seq models — a bidirectional GRU encoder, a GRU decoder, the same sizes and the same 1,200 steps — one with a fixed context vector, one with Bahdanau attention.

How to read the results:

- **The fixed-vector model degrades with length**, exactly as Cho et al. saw on real translation: perfect at 3 letters, 77% at 7, 13% at 10 — even though 10 is inside its training range. One vector can't hold ten letters in order.
- **The attention model is perfect on every length it trained on**, and still gets 86% of 12-letter strings it has never seen. It doesn't need to memorise the string; it only needs to know *where to look next*.
- **The weights form the anti-diagonal.** Each output letter puts most of its weight on the source letter it copies. The weight is shared with a neighbour because each annotation $h_j$ comes from a bidirectional RNN and already carries information about adjacent letters.
- **It isn't magic.** At 14 letters even the attention model falls to 5%. The decoder's recurrent state has never had to count that far. Generalising to longer inputs than you trained on remains hard, and it comes back in Chapter 14.

**1. Simpler score functions.** The additive score needs a small neural network per pair. Luong compared it with cheaper alternatives:

$$
\text{score}(s_t, h_j) =
\begin{cases}
v_a^{\top} \tanh\!\left(W_a [s_t ; h_j]\right) & \text{concat (additive)}\\[4pt]
s_t^{\top} W_a\, h_j & \text{general (bilinear)}\\[4pt]
s_t^{\top} h_j & \text{dot}
\end{cases}
$$

The **dot** score has no parameters at all and turns scoring every position into one matrix multiplication — the operation GPUs are fastest at.

![Four score functions side by side in the hand-drawn style: additive (Bahdanau), general and dot (Luong), and scaled dot (Vaswani 2017). Each box shows its formula, parameter count and cost. A note explains that as the dimension d grows, dot products grow like √d, which saturates the softmax; dividing by √d keeps scores near unit size.](/appendix-c/11-attention-the-fix-for-the-bottleneck/score-functions.png)

**2. Attend with the current state.** Luong computes attention from $s_t$, the decoder state *after* this step's update, and then combines it: $\tilde{s}_t = \tanh\!\left(W_c [c_t ; s_t]\right)$, predicting from $\tilde{s}_t$. Bahdanau used $s_{t-1}$ and fed $c_t$ *into* the update. Both work; Luong's version is simpler to implement.

**3. Global vs local attention.** *Global* attention looks at every source word, as above. *Local* attention first predicts a centre position $p_t$ and attends only to a window $[p_t - D,\ p_t + D]$ around it, weighting by a Gaussian. It's cheaper on long inputs, and an early ancestor of today's sliding-window attention.

Luong also introduced **input feeding**: pass the previous $\tilde{s}_{t-1}$ into the next step's input, so the model knows what it attended to last time and doesn't translate the same word twice.

## Why attention also fixes the gradient problem

The bottleneck wasn't the only problem with RNNs. To learn that the first word affects the last, the gradient has to travel back through every step in between. By the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\partial h_n}{\partial h_1} = \prod_{k=2}^{n} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}
$$

That is a product of $n-1$ Jacobian matrices. If their typical size is a bit below 1, the product shrinks **exponentially** with $n$ — the vanishing gradient from Chapter 9. LSTMs and GRUs slow the decay, but they don't remove it.

Attention adds a **shortcut**. The context $c_t$ depends on every $h_j$ directly through one weighted sum, so there's always a path from any source position to any output that is a single step long, however far apart they are. The notebook measures this: it computes how much the last output of a sequence changes when you nudge the first input, for a vanilla RNN and for a single attention layer.

## Attention beyond translation

Once people saw that "learn where to look" worked for translation, it spread fast:

- **Show, Attend and Tell** (Xu et al., 2015) captioned images by attending over a grid of CNN features. The weights showed the model looking at the frisbee while it wrote "frisbee".
- **Pointer Networks** (Vinyals et al., 2015) used the attention weights themselves as the output — "point at input item $j$" — to solve sorting and convex-hull problems, and later to copy rare words in summarisation.
- **Speech recognition** — Listen, Attend and Spell (Chan et al., 2016) — aligned audio frames with characters the same way.
- **Memory networks and the Neural Turing Machine** (Graves et al., 2014) used content-based attention to read from an external memory, a close relative of the same idea.

### A caution: attention weights are not explanations

The alignment heatmaps are so readable that it's tempting to treat attention as the model's *reason*. Jain & Wallace ("Attention is not Explanation", 2019) showed that you can often swap a trained model's attention for a very different distribution and get the same prediction, so high weight doesn't prove a token mattered. Wiegreffe & Pinter ("Attention is not not Explanation", 2019) replied that adversarial weights are harder to find than that suggests, and that attention does carry real signal. The fair summary: attention weights show where information was *read from*, not *why* the model decided. Proper attribution needs other tools, such as gradients or ablations — Chapter 13 returns to this with interpretability research on heads.

## What attention fixed — and what it didn't

![Summary panel in hand-drawn style. Left: a table of three problems (fixed bottleneck, vanishing long-range gradients, sequential computation) and how seq2seq, Bahdanau attention and the 2017 Transformer each fare. Middle: a bar chart of BLEU scores, RNNencdec-30 13.93, RNNsearch-30 21.50, RNNencdec-50 17.82, RNNsearch-50 26.75. Right: the complexity table from Vaswani et al. — recurrent layers O(n·d²) compute, O(n) sequential steps, O(n) path length; self-attention O(n²·d), O(1), O(1) — with a sketch of path lengths.](/appendix-c/11-attention-the-fix-for-the-bottleneck/what-attention-fixed.png)

Bahdanau attention fixed the **information bottleneck** and gave gradients a **shortcut**. It didn't fix the third problem: the encoder and decoder were still RNNs. Step $t$ still waited for step $t-1$, so a 50-word sentence took 50 sequential steps however many GPU cores you had. As datasets grew to billions of words, that serial loop was the limit.

The obvious next question was the one Vaswani et al. asked in 2017: if attention is doing the useful work, why keep the RNN at all? Let every position attend to every other position — the source to itself, the target to itself — and process all of them at once. The table from their paper makes the trade-off precise ($n$ = sequence length, $d$ = width):

| layer type | compute per layer | sequential steps | longest path between two tokens |
|---|---|---|---|
| recurrent | $O(n \cdot d^2)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| self-attention | $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |

Self-attention pays for comparing all pairs — quadratic in $n$ — but every position is computed in parallel and any two tokens are one step apart. For sentences, where $n$ is usually smaller than $d$, it is even cheaper. That bet is "Attention Is All You Need", and it is what the rest of this appendix unpacks, starting with self-attention in Chapter 12.

## Quick recap

- The seq2seq **bottleneck** forced a whole sentence into one vector, and quality fell with length.
- **Bahdanau attention** keeps every encoder annotation $h_j$ and builds a fresh context $c_t = \sum_j \alpha_{tj} h_j$ at each step, with weights $\alpha_{tj} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(e_{tj})$ from a small learned score $e_{tj} = v_a^{\top}\tanh(W_a s_{t-1} + U_a h_j)$.
- The weights learn a **soft alignment** — including reorderings — with no alignment supervision. On WMT'14 English→French that was worth about 9 BLEU points.
- **Luong** added cheaper scores (general, dot), global vs local attention and input feeding. Dot-product scores grow like $\sqrt{d}$, which is why the Transformer scales them.
- Attention gives a **one-step gradient path** between any two positions, but attention weights are not proof of *why* a model decided something.
- What remained was the **sequential RNN**. Removing it entirely is the Transformer.
