# 14. Positional Encoding

"Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain exactly the same words. Only the order tells you who's in trouble. An RNN gets order for free, because it reads one word after another. Self-attention does not. It compares every token with every other token at once, and nothing in $\operatorname{softmax}(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d_k})\,V$ says *where* any token sits.

This chapter is about how Transformers get word order back. We'll prove that attention really is blind to order, build the original sinusoidal encoding and see why its waves were a clever choice, then follow the field to **RoPE** — the rotation trick used by nearly every modern LLM — and to the methods that let a model read far longer texts than it was trained on.

---

## The problem: attention can't see order

Shuffle the input tokens and self-attention gives you the same output vectors, shuffled the same way. Formally, for any permutation matrix $P$ (a reordering of rows):

$$
\operatorname{Attn}(PX) = P \operatorname{Attn}(X)
$$

The proof is three lines. The projections act on each row separately, so $Q' = PQ$, $K' = PK$, $V' = PV$. Then

$$
\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{PQ\,(PK)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) PV
= \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(P\,\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\,P^{\top}\right) PV
= P\,A\,P^{\top} P V = P\,(AV)
$$

using $P^{\top}P = I$ and the fact that a row-wise softmax doesn't care how rows and columns are ordered. This property is called **permutation equivariance**. The output vector for *dog* is identical whether the dog bites or is bitten; only its row moved. The feed-forward layers (Chapter 15) act on each token separately, so they are equivariant too. A Transformer without position information treats its input as a **bag of words**.

![Attention can't see word order. ### A nuance: causal decoders can partly find position on their own

The proof above is for attention without a mask. A **causal** decoder is not fully order-blind: token 5 sees five tokens, token 50 sees fifty, and the model can learn to read that — for instance, the attention a token pays to the first token shrinks roughly as $1/(\text{position})$. Haviv et al. (2022) and Kazemnejad et al. (2023, "NoPE") showed that decoder-only models with **no positional encoding** at all still learn usable position information, and sometimes generalise to longer inputs better than models with one. Still, every major model uses an explicit method, because it makes position easy to learn and precise.

## Method 1: sinusoidal encoding (2017)

The original Transformer adds a fixed vector $\text{PE}(p)$ to the embedding of the token at position $p$. Each pair of dimensions is a sine and a cosine at a different frequency:

$$
\text{PE}(p, 2i) = \sin\!\left(p \,\omega_i\right), \qquad
\text{PE}(p, 2i+1) = \cos\!\left(p \,\omega_i\right), \qquad
\omega_i = \frac{1}{10000^{2i/d}}
$$

for $i = 0, 1, \dots, d/2 - 1$. Pair 0 has frequency 1, so it repeats every $2\pi \approx 6.3$ tokens. Each later pair is slower, down to a wavelength of about $10000 \cdot 2\pi \approx 63{,}000$ tokens for the last pair.

Why waves? Think of a clock. The second hand alone can't tell you the time of day, but seconds, minutes and hours together give every moment a unique reading. Fast dimensions tell neighbouring tokens apart; slow dimensions tell paragraphs apart. It's like the bits of a binary counter, but smooth. And:

- every position gets a **unique** vector;
- every value stays in $[-1, 1]$, whatever the position;
- there are **no parameters** to learn, and any position can be computed, even one longer than anything seen in training.

The vector is **added** to the token embedding, not concatenated: both are $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional. That seems like it would garble the word's meaning, but in hundreds of dimensions the model can learn to keep "what" and "where" in mostly separate directions.

### The real reason for sines and cosines: shifts are rotations

Vaswani et al. chose this form because they hypothesised it would make **relative** positions easy to learn. Here's why. Write pair $i$ of $\text{PE}(p)$ as a 2-D point $u_i(p) = (\sin \omega_i p,\ \cos \omega_i p)$ on the unit circle. The angle-addition formulas give

$$
u_i(p + k) =
\begin{bmatrix} \cos \omega_i k & \sin \omega_i k \\ -\sin \omega_i k & \cos \omega_i k \end{bmatrix}
u_i(p)
$$

The matrix is a **rotation**, and it depends only on the offset $k$, not on $p$. Stack one rotation per pair into a block-diagonal matrix $M_k$, and

$$
\text{PE}(p + k) = M_k\, \text{PE}(p) \quad \text{for every } p
$$

"$k$ tokens later" is one fixed linear map, which is exactly the kind of thing attention's linear projections can learn. A consequence: the dot product of two position vectors depends only on their distance,

$$
\text{PE}(p) \cdot \text{PE}(p + k) = \sum_{i} \cos(\omega_i k)
$$

It peaks at $k = 0$ and falls off with distance. But it's symmetric in $k$, so on its own it can't tell *before* from *after*.

## Method 2: learned absolute positions (2018)

The simplest alternative is to drop the formula and **learn** a vector for each position, exactly like a word embedding: a table with one row per position. BERT, GPT-2 and GPT-3 did this. GPT-2 small's table is $1{,}024 \times 768 = 786{,}432$ parameters.

Learned positions work about as well as sinusoids within the trained range (Vaswani et al. reported nearly identical results). Their weakness is a hard limit: there is simply no row for position 1,025. A model trained on 1,024 tokens cannot read 1,025 without retraining. And because position is mixed into the token vectors at the input, every layer has to carry it along through the residual stream.

---

## Method 3: relative position — put distance into the scores

What usually matters in language is not "this word is at position 37" but "this word is **three tokens before** that one". So a line of work stopped adding position to the input and instead put the *distance* between two tokens directly into their attention score:

- **Shaw et al. (2018)** added a learned vector for each clipped distance $j - i$ to the keys (and values);
- **Transformer-XL (Dai et al., 2019)** rewrote the score into content and position terms with sinusoidal relative distances;
- **T5 (Raffel et al., 2020)** used the simplest version: a single learned scalar bias per distance bucket and head, added to the score — finer buckets for nearby tokens and logarithmically wider ones far away.

These made "three tokens back" mean the same thing everywhere in the sequence. The method that won combines that idea with the rotation property above.

---

## Method 4: RoPE — rotary position embedding (2021)

**RoPE** (Su et al., 2021, "RoFormer") doesn't add anything to the embeddings. Inside every attention layer, it **rotates** each query and key by an angle proportional to its position, just before the dot product.

Take one 2-D pair of a query and key. For the query at position $m$ and the key at position $n$:

$$
\tilde{q}_m = R(m\theta)\, q, \qquad \tilde{k}_n = R(n\theta)\, k, \qquad
R(\phi) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\ \sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{bmatrix}
$$

Rotations compose by adding angles, and the transpose of a rotation is the rotation backwards: $R(a)^{\top} R(b) = R(b - a)$. So the attention score is

$$
\tilde{q}_m^{\top} \tilde{k}_n = q^{\top} R(m\theta)^{\top} R(n\theta)\, k = q^{\top} R\big((n - m)\theta\big)\, k
$$

The absolute positions cancel and **only the offset $n - m$ survives**. Rotate both vectors by the same extra amount and the angle between them — and so their dot product — doesn't change. Unlike the plain sinusoidal dot product, $R\big((n-m)\theta\big)$ is not symmetric, so the score can tell before from after.

A head of size $d$ is split into $d/2$ pairs, each rotating at its own speed, with the same geometric frequencies as the sinusoids:

$$
\theta_i = \text{base}^{-2i/d}, \qquad i = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1
$$

The paper used base = 10,000; Llama 3 uses 500,000, so its slowest pairs turn even more slowly and stay distinguishable over longer contexts.

Why it won:

- **Relative by construction**, with no learned position parameters at all.
- **Applied in every layer** to Q and K only. Values aren't rotated, and the residual stream stays free of position.
- **Works with the KV cache**: a key is rotated once, by its own position, when it's written, and never needs updating.
- **Long-range decay**: averaged over directions, the score between two tokens tends to shrink as they get further apart, a sensible bias for language.

## Method 5: ALiBi — a penalty for distance (2022)

**ALiBi** (Press, Smith & Lewis, 2022, "Train Short, Test Long") goes further: no position vectors and no rotations. It just subtracts a penalty from each attention score that grows linearly with distance:

$$
\text{score}_{ij} = \frac{q_i \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d_k}} - m_h \,(i - j) \qquad (j \le i)
$$

Each head $h$ gets its own fixed slope $m_h$, a geometric sequence. For 8 heads they are $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \dots, \tfrac{1}{256}$, so some heads focus sharply on nearby tokens while others look far back. Because the penalty keeps growing smoothly past the training length, ALiBi models trained on 1,024 tokens kept working on 2,048 and beyond with little loss. BLOOM and MPT used it.

## Reading past the training length

A model trained on 4,096 tokens usually breaks at 16,000. With learned positions there's no table entry. With RoPE, the slow pairs reach rotation angles they never saw in training, and attention scores become unpredictable. Since long context became a selling point, a family of fixes appeared for RoPE models:

- **Position interpolation** (Chen et al., 2023): divide every position by the extension factor — for 4× longer inputs, position 16,000 is treated as 4,000. All angles stay in the trained range, at the cost of squeezing neighbouring tokens closer together. A short fine-tune (about 1,000 steps) adapts the model.
- **NTK-aware scaling** (2023): instead of squeezing all frequencies equally, raise the RoPE base. That stretches the slow, long-range pairs a lot and leaves the fast, local pairs nearly untouched, so nearby tokens stay sharp.
- **YaRN** (Peng et al., 2023): treat each frequency band differently — leave fast pairs alone, interpolate slow ones, blend in between — and slightly adjust the softmax temperature. It extended Llama 2 to 128k tokens using roughly 0.1% of the original pretraining data.

The same idea shows up in pretraining: Llama 3 uses base 500,000 from the start, and long-context models finish pretraining with a stretch of long documents.

## Quick recap

- Self-attention is **permutation-equivariant**: $\operatorname{Attn}(PX) = P\operatorname{Attn}(X)$. Without position information a Transformer sees a bag of words. A causal mask leaks some order, but not reliably.
- **Sinusoidal** encoding adds $\sin(p\,\omega_i)$ and $\cos(p\,\omega_i)$ waves at geometric frequencies $\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d}$. Shifting by $k$ is a fixed rotation, $\text{PE}(p+k) = M_k\,\text{PE}(p)$.
- **Learned absolute** positions are simple but capped at the trained length.
- **Relative** methods put the distance $j - i$ straight into the attention score (Shaw, Transformer-XL, T5).
- **RoPE** rotates queries and keys by their positions, so $\tilde{q}_m^{\top}\tilde{k}_n = q^{\top}R\big((n-m)\theta\big)k$ depends only on the offset. No parameters, works with the KV cache, used by nearly every modern LLM.
- **ALiBi** subtracts a per-head linear distance penalty and extrapolates gracefully.
- **Position interpolation, NTK scaling and YaRN** stretch trained RoPE models to much longer contexts.
