# 18. Alignment — RLHF and DPO

After SFT, the model follows instructions. That's genuinely useful. But SFT has a problem it can't solve on its own: it teaches the model to *imitate* the training data, and training data is never perfectly curated. The model can imitate a mediocre response just as easily as a great one, because the loss function doesn't distinguish — it just measures "how well did the model predict this specific sequence of tokens?"

What you actually want is for the model to *prefer* better responses over worse ones. That requires a fundamentally different training signal: human judgments about which response is better.

This is what **alignment training** does.

---

## The Problem: Pre-training and SFT Miss Human Preferences

Here's what a raw SFT model can still do wrong:

```text
Prompt: "Should I take ibuprofen with alcohol?"

SFT model (imitating training data):
  "Some people take ibuprofen after drinking. The combination can
   cause stomach issues in some individuals."

Desired response:
  "No — combining ibuprofen with alcohol significantly increases
   the risk of stomach bleeding and liver damage. Avoid this."

The SFT model isn't lying. It's completing a plausible pattern.
But the second response is safer and more helpful.
No amount of next-token prediction loss tells the model this.
```

To fix this, you need training examples that say: "this response is better than that one." You need **preference data**.

---

## The RLHF Pipeline

**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)** is the training pipeline used by OpenAI (ChatGPT), Anthropic (Claude), and others. It has three stages:

```text
Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)
   ↓
   Take the base model, fine-tune on curated instruction-response pairs
   (Chapter 16). Output: SFT Model.

Stage 2: Train a Reward Model (RM)
   ↓
   Collect human preference data: (prompt, chosen response, rejected response)
   Train a separate model that predicts which response humans prefer.
   Output: Reward Model — a function that scores any (prompt, response) pair.

Stage 3: PPO — Fine-tune with Reinforcement Learning
   ↓
   Use the Reward Model as the "environment".
   Update the SFT model to generate responses that get higher reward scores.
   Output: Aligned Model.
```

---

## Stage 2: The Reward Model

The reward model takes a prompt and a response and outputs a single scalar score — higher means better.

```text
Input:
  Prompt:   "What's the capital of France?"
  Response: "Paris is the capital of France."

Output:   r = 8.4  (high score — good response)

─────────────────────────────────────────

Input:
  Prompt:   "What's the capital of France?"
  Response: "It depends on the context."

Output:   r = 1.2  (low score — evasive, unhelpful)
```

**Architecture:** Same transformer as the language model, but the LM head is replaced with a single linear layer that outputs one number instead of a vocabulary distribution.

**Training data:** Pairs of `(prompt, chosen, rejected)` collected from human labelers:

```text
Example preference pair:
  Prompt:   "Explain black holes in simple terms"
  Chosen:   "Black holes are regions where gravity is so strong
              that nothing, not even light, can escape. They form
              when massive stars collapse at the end of their lives."
  Rejected: "Black holes are quite complex astrophysical phenomena
              related to Einstein's general relativity equations..."
```

The reward model learns to assign a higher score to `chosen` than `rejected`.

### The Bradley-Terry Loss

The reward model is trained with the **Bradley-Terry** loss — a pairwise preference loss borrowed from sports ranking:

```text
Given:
  r_chosen   = reward model score for the chosen response
  r_rejected = reward model score for the rejected response

We want: r_chosen > r_rejected

Loss = -log( sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected) )
     = -log( sigma(r_chosen - r_rejected) )

Where sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + e^(-x))

Intuition:
  If r_chosen >> r_rejected → diff is large positive
    → sigmoid(large) ≈ 1
    → -log(1) ≈ 0   (low loss, good)

  If r_chosen ≈ r_rejected → diff ≈ 0
    → sigmoid(0) = 0.5
    → -log(0.5) ≈ 0.69  (medium loss)

  If r_chosen << r_rejected → diff is large negative
    → sigmoid(-large) ≈ 0
    → -log(0) → ∞   (huge loss, bad)
```

```text
Loss surface (Bradley-Terry):

  loss
   ^
   |  .
   | ..
   |   ..
   |     ...
   |        ........
   +──────────────────> r_chosen - r_rejected
   -8  -4   0   4   8

  Large negative diff = wrong ordering = high loss
  Large positive diff = correct ordering = low loss
  The model is trained to push chosen scores up and rejected scores down.
```

## Stage 3: PPO — Training With the Reward Signal

Once the reward model is trained, it becomes the environment for RL training. The language model is the **policy** — it generates tokens, and the reward model judges the complete response.

### The PPO Objective

**PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)** is the RL algorithm used in RLHF. Its key innovation: it prevents the policy from updating too aggressively in one step, which would cause training instability.

```text
Two core components of PPO:

1. Policy ratio:
   ratio = π_new(token | prompt) / π_old(token | prompt)
           ^────────────────────   ^──────────────────────
           new policy probability  old policy probability

   ratio > 1: new policy assigns MORE probability to this token than old
   ratio < 1: new policy assigns LESS probability

2. Clipping:
   ratio_clipped = clip(ratio, 1-ε, 1+ε)  where ε ≈ 0.2

   This limits how much the policy can change in one step:
     ratio > 1.2 gets clipped to 1.2
     ratio < 0.8 gets clipped to 0.8

PPO Loss = -E[ min(ratio × A, clip(ratio, 1-ε, 1+ε) × A) ]
                  ^──────────  ^──────────────────────────
                  unclipped     clipped version

Take the MINIMUM of both → conservative update → stable training
A = advantage = reward_score - baseline  (how much better than average)
```

```text
PPO clipping visualization (ε=0.2, advantage=+1.0):

  objective
  (what we maximize)
   ^
1.2|              *──────────────  ← clipped at 1+ε=1.2
   |           *
   |        *
1.0|     *
   |  *
0.8|*
   |
   +──────────────────────> policy ratio
   0.5   1.0   1.5   2.0

If ratio > 1.2: objective is capped at 1.2 (no benefit from larger updates)
This stops the model from making huge jumps that might destabilize training.
```

### The KL Penalty

There's a second term in the full PPO loss: a **KL divergence penalty** between the current policy and the original SFT model:

```text
Full PPO objective:
  L = E[min(ratio × A, clip(ratio, 1-ε, 1+ε) × A)]  ← PPO term
    - β × KL(π_current || π_SFT)                      ← KL penalty

  where β is a small coefficient (0.01 to 0.1)

The KL penalty prevents "reward hacking":
  Without KL: the model might find adversarial inputs to the reward model
  — outputs that score very high but are actually terrible text
  (e.g., repeating a single token endlessly if the RM rewards length)

  With KL: staying close to the SFT model is rewarded
  — extreme deviations get penalized
```

## DPO: Throwing Away the Reward Model

RLHF works, but it's expensive and fiddly:
- You need to train *and maintain* a separate reward model
- PPO training is unstable and hard to tune
- The reward model can be gamed (reward hacking)

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO, Rafailov et al. 2023)** eliminates all of this. The key insight: through a mathematical transformation, the RLHF objective can be expressed directly in terms of the language model probabilities — no separate reward model needed.

### The DPO Loss

```text
DPO objective:

  L_DPO = -log( sigma(
    beta * log( pi_theta(chosen) / pi_ref(chosen) )
    -
    beta * log( pi_theta(rejected) / pi_ref(rejected) )
  ))

Where:
  pi_theta = the model being trained (current policy)
  pi_ref   = the reference SFT model (frozen, not updated)
  beta     = temperature parameter (controls aggressiveness, typically 0.1–0.5)
  sigma    = sigmoid function

Simplified view:
  chosen_improvement   = log pi_theta(chosen)   - log pi_ref(chosen)
  rejected_improvement = log pi_theta(rejected)  - log pi_ref(rejected)

  L_DPO = -log sigma( beta * (chosen_improvement - rejected_improvement) )
```

The intuition:

```text
DPO wants:
  chosen_improvement   > 0   → pi_theta assigns MORE prob to chosen than pi_ref does
  rejected_improvement < 0   → pi_theta assigns LESS prob to rejected than pi_ref does

  The loss is low when chosen gets relatively more probable AND rejected gets
  relatively less probable — both measured against the reference model.

  pi_ref acts as a regularizer: the model shouldn't change too drastically
  from the SFT checkpoint (prevents reward hacking via implicit KL).
```

## RLHF vs DPO: Which to Use?

```text
RLHF (PPO):
  + More flexible: reward model can be separately improved
  + Can use online RL: generate responses and score them in real time
  + Better for very large models when stability matters
  - Requires training & maintaining a separate reward model
  - PPO is notoriously unstable and hard to tune
  - Much more engineering complexity

DPO:
  + Much simpler: one training loop, no separate RM
  + More stable training
  + Works well on most alignment tasks
  - Offline only: uses fixed preference dataset (no online generation)
  - Beta requires tuning; sensitivity to reference model quality
  - Theoretically suboptimal for very large distribution shifts

In practice: Most teams now start with DPO.
InstructGPT (2022) used RLHF; LLaMA-2/3-chat, Mistral-Instruct, and
most recent open models use DPO or variants.
```

## What Alignment Actually Achieves

Before alignment, a model trained purely on SFT produces plausible responses — it imitates the data. After alignment:

```text
Prompt: "My code has a bug. Here's the function: [code]"

SFT only:                        After RLHF/DPO alignment:
─────────────────────────────    ────────────────────────────────
"Here's the function: [code]     "I can see the issue — on line 3,
 which has a potential issue.     you're comparing `x` with `==`
 The code might have bugs in      instead of `is`, which fails for

                                  This also handles the edge case
                                  where x is an empty list."

SFT learned: responses about     Alignment learned: users prefer
bugs sound like this.             responses that actually fix bugs.
```

The model learned to be *more useful*, not just *more fluent*. That's the gap alignment closes.

## Quick Recap

- **SFT** trains on curated examples but can't distinguish good from mediocre responses — it imitates patterns.
- **RLHF** (3 stages): SFT → Reward Model trained on `(prompt, chosen, rejected)` triplets → PPO uses the RM as a reward signal to update the policy.
- **Reward Model (Bradley-Terry)**: Loss = `-log(sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected))`. Trains the RM to assign higher scores to human-preferred responses.
- **PPO**: Ratio = `π_new / π_old`. Clipped to `[1-ε, 1+ε]` to prevent destabilizing large updates. KL penalty prevents reward hacking.
- **DPO**: Directly optimizes on preference data without a separate RM. Loss = `-log(sigma(beta * (chosen_improvement - rejected_improvement)))`. Simpler, more stable, now preferred by most practitioners.
- Both RLHF and DPO use the **SFT model as a reference** — the aligned model should not deviate too far from it (prevents degenerate outputs).

## The Complete Math of RLHF and DPO

**The Bradley-Terry reward model:**
To learn which responses humans prefer, you train a reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ that scores responses. With preference pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$, the Bradley-Terry model says:

$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))$$

Training objective:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))$$

Intuition: train the model to assign higher reward to preferred responses. Sigmoid converts the score difference to a probability; log-likelihood training maximizes correct preference predictions.

**The RLHF objective — reward + KL constraint:**

$$\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[r_\phi(x, y)] - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$$

Two competing forces:
- Maximize reward (generate responses humans prefer)
- Stay close to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (don't drift too far from the original model)

The $\beta$ coefficient controls this tradeoff. High $\beta$ = conservative (don't change much). Low $\beta$ = aggressive (chase reward more). The KL term is essential — without it, the model would learn to "hack" the reward model with outputs that score high but are nonsensical.

**PPO — the gradient update rule:**
PPO solves this with a clipped objective:

$$\rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}$$

$$L = \mathbb{E}\left[\min\left(\rho_t A_t, \text{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t\right)\right]$$

The clip prevents any single update from changing the policy too much. If $\rho_t > 1 + \epsilon$, the gradient is cut off — don't let this step dominate. This is what makes PPO stable on long training runs.

**DPO — skipping the reward model entirely:**
DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) is mathematically elegant: it rearranges the RLHF objective to directly optimize preferences without ever training a reward model.

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\left(\beta \left[\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\right]\right)$$

Interpretation: increase the model's relative probability of preferred responses ($y_w$) and decrease it for rejected responses ($y_l$), all relative to the reference model. No explicit reward model, no RL loop. Just gradient descent on paired preferences.

**Goodhart's Law and alignment tax:**
If your reward model $r_\phi$ isn't perfectly aligned with true human preferences $r^*$:

$$r_\phi \neq r^*$$

Then maximizing $r_\phi$ will eventually diverge from maximizing $r^*$. The model learns to game the proxy. This is Goodhart's Law in action: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Post-training can also introduce an "alignment tax" — tradeoffs where making a model safer or more helpful in one dimension hurts other capabilities: calibration, factual diversity, reasoning on unusual topics.
